Where AI tools actually help a small business right now — and where they don't, yet.

"AI" covers wildly different things: a writing assistant, a customer-service chatbot, a document-automation pipeline, a fully autonomous agent. They don't have remotely the same cost, setup effort, or payoff. This is a plain-language rundown by category, including where the juice genuinely isn't worth the squeeze yet.

Good fit today

Writing, editing, and everyday communication

Drafting emails, cleaning up written documents, summarizing long threads, or getting a first pass at a proposal. Low setup cost, immediate time savings, low risk if a human still reviews the output before it goes out the door.

Workable, with real setup effort

Customer-facing chatbots and support automation

Can genuinely cut response time and staff workload, but only pays off once it's actually configured around your specific products, policies, and edge cases. A chatbot that gives wrong answers to customers does more damage than having no chatbot at all — this is the category where sloppy setup costs the most.

Good ROI at volume

Document processing and data entry automation

Pulling structured data out of invoices, forms, or receipts at scale can pay for itself quickly — but only if you're processing enough volume to justify the setup. For a handful of documents a week, the manual process is often still cheaper than the tooling and oversight it takes to automate it properly.

Usually premature

"Replace a role with AI" broad automation

The most heavily marketed promise and the least reliable one for a business your size. These systems generally need ongoing technical oversight, guardrails, and error-correction that a small operation isn't staffed to provide — the hidden cost is the maintenance, not the subscription.

Most common outcome

A short list of specific tasks worth automating now, and a longer list worth revisiting in a year

Almost no small business needs an "AI strategy." Most need two or three well-chosen tools solving specific, recurring bottlenecks — and an honest sense of which flashier options to skip for now. That short list is the actual deliverable of an assessment.

The part most AI pitches skip

What happens to your data the moment you paste it into an AI tool.

Before picking a tool, it's worth knowing what you're agreeing to. This isn't about being anti-AI — it's about not signing away customer or business data without realizing it.

Lower risk

Business-tier tools with a written no-training data policy

  • Vendor contractually excludes your data from model training
  • Clear data-retention and deletion terms
  • Admin controls over who on your team can use it
  • Reasonable fit for most day-to-day business use
Read the terms first

Free consumer versions of the same tools

  • Often reserve the right to use your input for training
  • Fine for general drafting with no sensitive details
  • Not the place to paste customer records, financials, or contracts

A one-time policy check per tool avoids most of the risk here — this is a five-minute part of an assessment, not a separate ordeal.

Avoid for sensitive data

Pasting client records, health, financial, or legal information into any general-purpose chat tool

Even with a good data policy, regulated data (health records, financial account details, anything covered by industry-specific privacy law) generally shouldn't go into a general AI tool without a specific compliance-grade agreement in place. If this applies to your business, it's worth a dedicated privacy and cybersecurity conversation before any AI tooling decision — a natural add-on to an assessment, not a prerequisite for having one.

Choosing a tool or vendor

Off-the-shelf tool, or something custom-built? And what to check before you sign up.

Once you know which task is worth automating, the next decision is what to actually buy or build — and AI vendor contracts have their own set of traps, separate from the usual software-subscription fine print.

Off-the-shelf usually wins when...

Your use case is common

  • Writing, summarizing, basic customer support
  • A mainstream tool already covers your workflow
  • You want to start now, not in three months
  • Low budget for setup and no in-house technical staff
Custom-built is worth considering when...

Your workflow is genuinely unusual

  • No off-the-shelf tool fits your specific process
  • High enough volume to justify setup and maintenance cost
  • You have (or will hire) someone to maintain it long-term
  • The task is stable enough not to need constant rebuilding
Common ways AI vendor contracts go wrong

Worth reading closely before you commit budget or business data to any AI vendor:

  • Unclear terms on whether your input data is used to train the vendor's models
  • Per-seat or usage-based pricing that scales unpredictably as your team grows
  • No clear data export or deletion path if you decide to switch tools later
  • Marketing that implies capabilities the actual product doesn't reliably deliver
  • Long annual contracts for a category of tool that's still changing every few months
