Windows 10 is past end-of-support. Here's what that actually means for your options.

"End of support" doesn't mean your computers stop working. It means Microsoft has stopped patching security holes in Windows 10, which means every month that passes without a plan is a month of accumulating risk. You have real choices — this is a plain-language rundown of each one, including the honest downsides.

Higher risk

Do nothing, stay on Windows 10

Your machines keep running, but any newly discovered security flaw in Windows 10 stays unpatched permanently. Over time this raises real exposure — to malware, to compliance requirements in regulated industries, and potentially to cyber-insurance claims being denied if an incident is traced to unsupported software.

Bridge option

Pay for Extended Security Updates

Microsoft sells a temporary bridge: continued critical security patches for Windows 10 for a fee, for a limited number of years, with pricing that increases each year you renew. It buys time — it doesn't solve the underlying problem, and it doesn't include bug fixes, new features, or general support.

Conventional path

Upgrade to Windows 11, on new hardware if needed

If your current machines meet Windows 11's hardware requirements (notably a TPM 2.0 security chip), the upgrade itself is typically free. If they don't, this path means new hardware. It's the path most vendors will default to recommending, for obvious reasons — it's also the one with the highest ongoing licensing and hardware-refresh costs, and includes the parts of Windows 11 that get the most complaints: bundled AI features, ads in the OS, telemetry.

Where we can help most

Migrate some or all machines to Linux and open source software

For a large share of everyday office work — browsing, email, document editing, video calls — a Linux desktop with open source applications can replace Windows entirely, on hardware that's several years old, at no licensing cost. It's not the right fit for every machine or every piece of specialized software, which is exactly what a readiness consult is for.

Most common outcome

A mix: some machines move, some stay on Windows, and you get outside help for the parts you can't do yourself

Very few small businesses land on one clean answer. A typical outcome is a handful of machines running specialized Windows-only software staying put (properly hardened and managed), general-purpose machines migrating to Linux, and a plan for whoever needs to physically touch hardware on-site — which is where good, honestly-priced local IT help matters, and where a vetting review before you sign anything can save real money.

Straight talk on Linux specifically

Linux isn't for everyone. Here's exactly who it's for.

If the migration path above looks like a fit, this is where it gets specific. No pitch — a plain breakdown of what moves cleanly, what needs a workaround, and what should just stay on Windows.

We'll walk through the migration and the alternatives on a call — often you'll save money on software licensing too.

Keep Windows

Some machines need to stay on Windows — and that's fine.

Industry-specific software — certain medical records systems, legal case management, CAD tools, specialized production equipment drivers — often has no viable Linux alternative. Part of a readiness consult is identifying those machines upfront and being plain about it, rather than pretending everything can move.

The user experience

It can look and feel like Windows. That's deliberate.

The biggest objection to Linux is usually "my staff will hate it." In practice, Linux desktops can be configured with a familiar taskbar, Start-menu-style launcher, and system tray — close enough to Windows that most staff adjust within a day or two. Which specific setup fits best depends on your hardware and team, and that's part of what a readiness consult sorts out — not something to figure out in advance.

When you need hands-on help

Doing it yourself, hiring outside help, or something in between.

Migrations and ongoing IT support often need someone physically at your location — swapping drives, running cable, fixing a printer. We don't do on-site work outside our own local area, but we can help you figure out whether you need it at all, and how to avoid the ways businesses commonly get overcharged or locked in when they hire a managed service provider (an outside firm that handles your IT on an ongoing basis, usually called an MSP for short).

DIY often works when...

Your team can handle it in-house

  • You have at least one comfortable, patient tech-user on staff
  • Machine count is small (roughly under 10)
  • No specialized on-site networking or server work involved
  • You can tolerate a slower, self-paced rollout
Outside help makes sense when...

You need hands physically on the hardware

  • Server, networking, or wiring work on-site
  • Larger fleets needing coordinated rollout
  • No internal staff with the time or comfort level
  • Ongoing monitoring/support you don't want to manage yourself
Common ways provider contracts go wrong

None of these mean a provider is acting in bad faith by default — but each is worth reading closely before signing:

  • Multi-year contracts with automatic renewal clauses and steep early-termination fees
  • Vague per-device or per-user pricing that balloons once you're a few months in
  • Bundled "security" or "compliance" add-ons priced well above their actual cost
  • Provider retains sole admin access to your accounts, domains, or backups — making it hard to leave even if you want to
  • No clearly defined service-level agreement (response time, scope of what's covered, what counts as an extra billable incident)

♻️

Keep hardware out of the landfill.

Electronic waste is one of the fastest-growing waste streams in the world. When Microsoft declares a four or five year old machine "incompatible" with Windows 11, the implicit pressure is to throw it away and buy something new — even though most of it still works fine.

Extending the working life of existing hardware by even three to five years with Linux keeps those machines productive and reduces the environmental cost of manufacturing replacements. It's one of the more straightforward green IT decisions a small business can make.
