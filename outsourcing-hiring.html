Deciding whether to Outsource or Hire in-house

This can be quite a tough decision for small businesses and nonprofit organizations to make. For an office with less than ten users, hiring an in-house technician might seem like overkill, or at least an unjustifiable expense. At the same time, outsourcing to an IT provider can quickly rack up huge bills and often the provider doesn't present their charges in a very transparent manner. Contract lock-in is another major cause for concern. And what if the IT provider you hire hoards all your administrative and server login information and passwords? In that case, changing providers becomes racked with fear and anxiety.

When in-house makes sense

An in-house IT hire might seem like a huge budget expenditure at first. There is probably at least one employee in your office who is somewhat tech-savvy; enough maybe to handle basic troubleshooting tasks. That person might even be you. But when computer problems start piling up, you or your tech-savvy assistant might end up overwhelmed and taken away from core job duties. Minor tech problems can quickly escalate into major workflow bottlenecks, resulting in many hours of lost productivity. Whole projects can come to a screeching halt.

Having an in-house IT technician on your team will ensure that minor tech problems are effectively nipped in the bud whenever they do pop up. Everyone else in the office can go back to doing their jobs and not worry about having to constantly put out digital fires.

The IT expertise hierarchy is actually very simple and not some great mystery it's often made out to be. Like with the trades, it's typically a 3-fold structure:

L1

General IT Technician

These are entry-level techs on the frontlines of technical support. L1s possess a lot of general computing knowledge and can tackle most basic-to-intermediate troubleshooting tasks. In a more regimented organization they will work from an extensive company knowledge base and series of scripts. They will carefully log and document every troubleshooting and support step they are carrying out from the initial help desk request to the final resolution, using a digital trouble-ticket system. L1s earn the lowest on the IT payscale, making them quite an affordable option for servicing small operations, especially when hired on a part-time or contractual basis.

L2

Specialist IT Technician

These are seasoned technicians who usually specialize in one or two particular areas of the IT world (like network administration, or software development, or cybersecurity, as examples). When an L1 is unable to resolve a complicated tech problem, the ticket is then escalated to an L2 tech. L2s aren't bogged down with simple and repetitive tasks, thus they have ample time to tackle the bigger problems and issues that require a lot more in-depth knowledge than an L1 is typically able to provide. An L2 is usually unnecessary and too costly in a small office environment. Thankfully, with rapid advancements in automation technology, an L1 can easily utilize AI tools to draw from L2 and L3 knowledge and expertise. When L2s are needed in-person, they will typically be with an outsourced provider, which we will further discuss below.

L3

Engineer

This is the expert, mastery level of the tech world. Engineers understand whole systems within their specialties and how and why those systems function from the architecture-level on-down. A small organization like yours will never require the expertise of an engineer for day-to-day, in-house technical support duties. When you do need an engineer it will be for major projects like designing and setting up an internal office network or performing a major cybersecurity audit. It's in these situations exactly where outsourcing to an IT provider becomes a viable and necessary option. Since engineers command the highest salaries in this hierarchy, a small company should only employ their services when absolutely necessary.

Outsourcing

So now we have arrived at a key insight. That is, when your company or organization needs L2 and L3 skills and expertise, outsourcing is very often the best way forward. Just to recap, that L1 generalist is great to have around your office (full or part time) for keeping things running smoothly on the computing end so everyone else on your team can be productive and worry-free when it comes to tech problems. But when there is a major problem or when there is a need for a large-scale project or migration, then bringing in the greater degree of expertise an outsourced IT provider can offer is a necessity. But before pulling the trigger on bringing in any particular provider, there are a few key things to keep in mind and a few red flags to look out for:

Pricing that isn't itemized is an immediate red flag. And look for any signs of vague per-device or per-user bundles that could easily balloon later.

Contract lock-in — You need to be able to get a good feel for a particular IT provider before you can be sure that they are both reputable and a good fit for your organization's needs. This means that multi-year terms will prevent you from being able to quickly back out of a bad arrangement. Auto-renewal and steep early-termination penalties are other questionable things to look for.

Credential hostage-taking happens when a provider holds sole admin/login access to your servers, cloud services, and any root admin or recovery accounts that may be on your company's workstations. This makes it very hard to leave that provider, should you opt to do so. Getting locked out of your servers and cloud accounts is no fun!

Undefined scope — This means there is no clear SLA (Service Level Agreement) on response time, or an unclear line between "included" and "billable extra." What you do NOT want to happen is your organization having an IT emergency that needs to be addressed immediately and your outsourced IT provider can't make it out to your office until two weeks after the fact, despite the informal verbal assurance of lightning-fast response time the company gave you before signing on the dotted line. In those kinds of nightmare situations, the contract you signed starts looking really bad.

No exit plan — You may be wondering what happens to your systems, data, and documentation if the relationship ends. If the IT service provider lists no plan or details on what will happen should you decide to sever your relationship with them, then this is certainly a cause for concern. And if you ask them to provide such a plan and all you get are vague promises and nothing concrete in writing, it's time to move onto the next candidate.

Not an all-or-nothing decision to make

The decision whether to hire in-house or whether to outsource is not an all-or-nothing proposition. Having an in-house technician for routine maintenance tasks and putting out small fires, paired with outsourced support for major projects, could very well be the winning formula your business or organization needs.
